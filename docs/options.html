Study 01 · Derivatives pricing

One option, priced four ways, each checking the others.

Black-Scholes gives the exact answer in one line. Monte Carlo estimates it from random paths. Variance reduction and quasi-random sequences make the estimate converge faster. Below, all four run on the same contract, the error shrinks on a log scale as paths grow, and the whole thing is then held up against a real SPY option chain to see where the model itself breaks.

Reading the race. Top chart: absolute error vs the closed form on log scales - every estimator should fall as paths grow. Bottom chart: the estimate itself homing in on the orange closed-form line. Plain Monte Carlo falls like 1/√N, as the central limit theorem says it must. QMC falls almost like 1/N because the integrand is one-dimensional and smooth. That gap, three orders of magnitude at a million paths, is the entire point of the study.

03

Held against the real market

Black-Scholes assumes one flat volatility for every strike. The market disagrees. Inverting the same formula on a pinned snapshot of 89 liquid SPY calls gives the volatility skew: downside protection trades at a much higher implied vol than at-the-money options. The formula still inverts cleanly - my solver's implied vols match the provider's to a median of 0.69 vol points - but no single σ prices the chain.

StrikeMoneynessMarket midFlat-σ priceFlat - midMy IV

Why this matters. The left side of that curve is the market paying up for crash insurance - a smile Black-Scholes says should not exist. It exists because real returns have fat tails and volatility that clusters and jumps, which is exactly what study 03 tries to forecast. The pricer is right about its own math; the model underneath is what the market is arguing with.
